Recently, while I was drafting that case study on AI transformation for Malaysian SMEs, my boss (Tan Sri Andrew Sheng) specifically reminded me to write the report in plain language and avoid piling on technical jargon. His exact words were that deliberately throwing around professional terms only makes people from non-technical backgrounds feel talked down to, and a truly good case study must be completely grounded.

To give me a firsthand experience of how to break down and communicate extremely complex issues using fundamental common sense, he arranged for me to sit in on a closed-door online seminar with only about thirty participants. The theme was hardcore: Complex System Cycles and Thermodynamic Reality.

Looking at the participant list, I took a sharp breath. The virtual room gathered global top-tier experts in complexity science, social evolution, neuroscience, and leadership. The seminar was divided into two parts: the first half was a framework sharing, and the second half was a nearly hour-long, high-density Q&A exchange.

Actually, while observing this meeting, a question kept hovering in my mind: How on earth do these bigwigs from archaeology, neuroscience, complex systems, and macro-finance seamlessly communicate across completely unfamiliar professional barriers?

Later, I completely understood, and it exactly validated my boss's original intention of asking me to write the report in "plain language." The highest level of cross-disciplinary communication is definitely not about throwing obscure jargon at each other, but about "downscaling" all complex concepts into common sense, metaphors, and underlying physical laws that everyone can understand. Accustomed to finding answers in definitive logical models, I watched them use the most straightforward language to elegantly deconstruct macro-finance, imperial cycles, and thermodynamic laws into clear, underlying system architectures.

## **The Physical Motherboard and Psychological Vulnerabilities of the World**

![Image](/images/life/TSAS_WhyComplexSystemCycle.jpg)

Right from the start, the meeting didn't throw out any complex economic formulas; instead, it presented an extremely cold thermodynamic reality that everyone understands: without energy input, nothing runs. The modern economic system was broken down into a three-tier architecture in plain English: the physical real world, the psychological world perceived by humans, and the third world composed of extremely complex abstract concepts.

Historical empires or complex social systems are fundamentally built on basic physical resources like energy, water, and food. But human psychological perception has severely detached from actual physical reality today. This frantic expansion was vividly compared to a "Candy Floss" economy. There was absolutely no need to explain derivative leverage; everyone instantly had a picture in their minds of something that looks massive and extremely sweet, but is highly hollow inside and will rapidly collapse upon impact.

System collapses often stem from the arrogance of top-level designers. When the elite class becomes self-enclosed and excessively squeezes energy from the bottom to maintain their luxurious consumption, the entire system heads towards decline. This is like an out-of-control program suffering a memory leak, taking up more and more resources while running less and less efficiently until it hits the physical limit and completely crashes. Facing such a complex system full of unknown variables, the West is accustomed to exhausting computing power to find theoretical optimal solutions. However, the most effective strategy suggested by the seminar is actually returning to Wang Yangming's "unity of knowledge and action" by utilizing dynamic trial and error through action.

### **Piercing Abstract Consensus to Find Physical Constraints**
During the discussion, a scholar questioned the frequent use of "thermodynamics" to explain economic phenomena, arguing that applying this physical concept to a commercial society driven by human subjective desires seemed unrigorous. To explain this, instead of citing profound theories, the meeting directly used the example of spending tens of thousands of dollars on a Gucci bag to materialize human psychological hallucination. The scholar immediately countered that people buy Gucci bags purely for class signaling and social status, which is strictly the realm of social psychology and has nothing to do with physical energy.

However, the subsequent response demonstrated a dimension-reducing perspective. The discussion didn't get bogged down in psychological jargon but directly dragged the issue back to the most fundamental constraints. No matter how great the psychological vanity is, what is the prerequisite for completing the purchase? It's money, which is energy. Even if this is a consensus bubble purely on a psychological level, you must have real wealth or sufficient social credit to borrow that money to maintain this signal. Once you can't borrow money, or the market realizes that real-world interest rates have skyrocketed beyond affordability, this class signal propped up by psychological consensus will instantly collapse due to the loss of underlying energy injection. This made me suddenly realize that when we analyze business models, it is far too easy to get entangled in abstract vocabulary like user psychology or brand premium. But the mindset of top experts is always to trace the cable back to the source. No matter how fancy the superstructure is, you must ultimately find that hard baseline constrained by physical laws and real capital.

### **System Sensor Failure and the Qianlong Moment**
An expert shifted the topic to organizational architecture, pointing out that in the current complex environment, the problem often doesn't lie with the leaders but with the followership, as people have lost the psychological safety to provide genuine upward feedback.

To explain this highly abstract "complex adaptive leadership," a vivid Chinese historical metaphor was introduced. The hardest thing for an ancient advisor to do was to tell the decision-maker holding the power of life and death that resources are exhausted. When decision-makers cut off the true feedback loop, the system faces a "Qianlong Moment," much like how Emperor Qianlong arrogantly rejected the British envoy in 1793, immersed in the hallucination of a celestial empire, ultimately leading to systemic collapse. This is exactly equivalent to sensor failure in engineering. If the probe of a temperature and humidity sensor is blocked, no matter how powerful the central processor is, the output commands will inevitably be wrong. The same goes for organizational architecture. Judging whether a system is healthy often isn't about looking at how pretty its financial data is, but testing whether its negative feedback mechanism is unobstructed. A system that cannot hear bad news is actually just one step away from crashing.

### **Switching from Global Optimum to Local Robustness**
Someone asked how we should respond in this era of approaching thermodynamic limits and global fragmentation. Instead of using complex macroeconomic jargon, a very Darwinian, plain-spoken consensus emerged: Eat lunch or be lunch.

In the past, we were used to pursuing efficiency in the context of globalization, but the current reality is that multilateral institutions and superpowers no longer have spare energy to save everyone. The world is turning into a multi-node mesh network where no one will come to help you, meaning you must generate your own energy and learn to survive at the limits. In this situation, the objective function of system optimization must be decisively switched from pursuing high efficiency to being sufficiently resilient. This reminds me that when the network environment is extremely unstable, pursuing ultimate execution speed is meaningless. The core strategy now must be to increase redundancy. Even if it means sacrificing some efficiency, you must ensure you can survive extreme stress tests like outages or power failures.

### **Proactively Triggering Local Errors to Prevent Global Crashes**
Towards the end of the meeting, a strategy scholar shared an interesting deep observation. He mentioned that when handling super real estate bubbles like Evergrande, if we don't go through these growing pains, we won't be able to implement bottomless bailouts like in the past when facing a full-blown global economic crisis in the future.

The meeting interpreted this phenomenon from a macro-historical perspective. Historically, this behavior of wealthy elites corrupting the system and demanding the state to foot the bill in an attempt to be too big to fail is the classic beginning of a decline. Using bottomless money printing to cover up the problem is equivalent to magnifying a local issue into the malignant debt of the entire economy, ultimately exacting an incalculable price. This macro-level decision-making logic is exactly like the exception handling mechanism when writing code. The Western financial model tends to wrap all bugs in a larger framework as long as the program doesn't crash. But a longer-term, healthier system architecture mindset is that it's better to let a sub-module proactively throw an exception and completely shut down and reboot, rather than ever allowing toxic code to leak into the kernel process.

This closed-door meeting completely overturned the way I process complex information. I suddenly understood my boss's true intention in asking me to write the SME transformation report in plain language. True grounding isn't a compromise. It is having the translation ability, much like these top think tanks, to translate extremely complex system logic into a cotton candy, a Gucci bag, or a historical story. Facing a highly non-linear, noise-filled real world, do not blindly worship complex abstract formulas. Returning to common sense, using the most straightforward language to monitor energy flows, keeping sensors unobstructed, and possessing the resilience to tear everything down and restart at any time is the most elegant strategy to combat system entropy.
