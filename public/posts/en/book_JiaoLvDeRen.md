## **— Seeing Adult Loss of Control, Defense Mechanisms, and Margin of Error Through an Absurd Hostage Situation**

To be honest, when I first opened *Anxious People*, I had to read it with a lot of patience.

Author Fredrik Backman's early narrative is extremely erratic, even a bit neurotic. A terrible bank robbery, a group of weird hostages trapped together—everyone is complaining and struggling in their own world, doing absurd things that are hard to understand. When I first started reading, this fragmented chaos made it hard to get into the zone.

But as the story progressed to the middle and later stages, a huge sense of satisfaction kicked in. Those seemingly random chit-chats and absurd coincidences suddenly snapped perfectly into place like scattered puzzle pieces. When all the clues came full circle, accompanied by that "owee, so that's what it is!" realization popping up in my mind, I gradually realized: what this book is truly about isn't a crime at all, but rather us—this group of adults who look decent on the outside but are constantly on the verge of losing control.

## **1. Why Do We Crave "A Sense of Distance"?**

There is a financially independent elite in the book named Zara, who said something I found incredibly real: "In this crowded world, the most expensive thing you can buy is the distance between people."

The longer I've been in society, the more I understand this mentality. Many people strive for higher social status, or are willing to pay for more expensive cabin classes and more private restaurants, often not to show off privilege, but to exchange for the "right not to be disturbed."

This sense of distance makes people feel safe. Because the adult world is full of wear and tear, when your status or financial ability builds a boundary that keeps the outside world at a respectful distance, you are actually protecting your internal order. This isn't apathy; it's a rational defense mechanism—we need enough physical and psychological space to place our inescapable exhaustion.

## **2. Curing "Chaos with Chaos" is Actually a Deep Fear of "Making a Mistake"**

Another psychological portrayal of modern people in the book is also highly precise: "The most human of anxiety symptoms is that we always try to cure chaos with chaos."

In real life, when life is about to hit a brick wall and lose control, the first reaction of many people isn't to hit the brakes, but to accelerate with a fluke mentality, even making more absurd decisions. I used to think this behavior was unreasonable, but now I'm starting to understand that this tension stems from our extreme fear of "making mistakes."

This reminds me of our social environment. Under the discipline of typical Asian education and the mindset of worshipping the strong, we have been implanted with a "test-taker" mode since childhood, defaulting to the belief that life has a perfect Standard Answer. Thus, we are terrified of deviating from the track, terrified of messing up. Once we deviate, we feel an immense sense of guilt, even feeling like we've lost the whole game. Because we are so afraid of taking the wrong step, when we lose control, we can only use a greater chaos to cover up our current powerlessness.

## **3. So-called "Messing Up" is Just Eliminating Wrong Answers**

The sentence in the entire book that touched me the most is the author's gentle reminder: "You are not the sum of all your yesterday's mistakes."

This sentence, combined with my recent discussions with friends, has given me a brand new sense of self-reconciliation regarding "making mistakes."

Actually, this real world is mostly in a gray area, not black and white (either bad or good). We are fumbling through such a complex society, and the prospect is inherently chaotic. Since this is the case, is so-called "taking a wrong turn" or "messing up" really that scary?

Practically speaking, we've all experienced those reckless, stubborn teenage moments of not turning back until we hit the wall. Some paths don't work for some people but do for others, and I just stubbornly wanted to hit that wall myself—if once wasn't enough, then twice, three times, four times... But so what? In this process, we gave it our all for the outcome we desired. Bystanders might think it's stupid, but didn't we grow and learn through failing time and time again, which eventually built our resilience when facing life's hardships later on?

This is the sentence I want to write down the most after reading this book: **Life doesn't come with an instruction manual, and everyone is being a human for the first time.**

We don't need to carry too much guilt over occasionally messing things up. Messing up itself is an experience; it's our process of trial and error in this gray reality. To be able to carry this self-consistency, stop being too hard on ourselves, and keep looking forward is already pretty amazing.

## **Conclusion**

I highly appreciate Backman's writing, not just for his exquisite structure, but even more so because after coldly observing all the wretchedness of human nature, he still retains immense compassion.

At the end of the story, he gave all the characters in the book who messed up their lives a Happy Ending. The moment I saw the ending, as a reader, my heart felt so warm.

It was like a friend who understands all your anxieties telling you: life will eventually defeat us all, but before that happens, don't forget to forgive yourself. Tomorrow is another day to start over.
