As the most solid pillars behind WOU, the scope of their conversation was naturally very grand.

So, following the chronological order of the meetings over these few days, I recorded the cross-disciplinary associations that deeply inspired me.

### **1. The ASEAN System Architecture: Triggering "Emergence" Through Hedging and Friction**

Facing the two mountains of China and the US, what exactly is ASEAN's role? As a native Malaysian, I was actually nodding frantically offstage when the scholars deconstructed the "ASEAN philosophy" during the roundtable on the first morning. Because this is not just a grand geopolitical consideration, but the reality we breathe every single day.

ASEAN is entirely different from the EU; it has no unified currency, no unified finance, and even its political ideologies vary wildly, making it a structurally loose alliance. Here, Islamic culture, Indian culture, Western systems, and profound Chinese culture are like interfaces of different protocols plugged directly into the same motherboard.

In engineering, hard-wiring different communication protocols inevitably generates errors and gibberish; in real life, this convergence of diverse cultures indeed brings unavoidable ideological clashes and daily friction. But this is the most fascinating part: complex systems theory tells us that an overly stable, monolithic system is often a stagnant pool of water, whereas the friction and heat at the boundaries of a system are precisely the most fertile hotbed for the "emergence" of new things.

This seemingly internal friction is actually constantly breaking the rigid barriers of monolithic thinking. Those unexpected commercial sparks and localized mutations of technical applications are bred precisely in the collision and mutual compromise of these diverse cultures. ASEAN never seeks to hardcode a standard answer, but rather acts like a vibrant and divergent open-source community, relying on extreme "robustness" to maintain huge buffer zones and survival elasticity under the squeeze of two major powers.

### **2. From Individual Heroism to the "Swarm Intelligence" of the Supply Chain**

During the same roundtable, everyone also talked about expanding overseas. Many subconsciously think going overseas is a kind of "physical translation": moving factories with excess domestic capacity over intact and slapping on a local brand to bypass trade barriers. But Chairman Sheng mentioned that this simple model is unviable and easily encounters look-through regulatory scrutiny.

What is truly valuable is exporting "Engineering Intelligence" (EI). The core competitiveness of China's manufacturing industry has never been birthing lone geniuses who disrupt the world single-handedly like Steve Jobs. Instead, it is the collective wisdom accumulated bit by bit by millions of engineers in a massive supply chain system through countless practical trials, errors, and extreme squeezes on cost and efficiency.

From the perspective of complex systems and psychology, this is an incredibly massive and hard-to-replicate "swarm intelligence". The West praises individual disruptive innovation, while the East's absolute advantage lies in highly coordinated, system-level innovation. Enterprises venturing overseas do so not just to leverage local low costs and multilingual advantages, but to unreservedly inject this EI into Malaysia's existing industrial foundation. Going overseas is not simple code copy-pasting, but a deep fusion and retraining of two different cultural "neural networks", using overflowing swarm intelligence to spawn brand new localized applications in a foreign land.

### **3. When the Loss Function of the "Free Market" is Modified to "National Security"**

Always, our highest principles in engineering are "efficiency" and "cost". I thought globalization was the same: wherever making chips is cheaper, the supply chain is most complete, and production efficiency is highest, capital and industry would automatically flow there like water. This used to be the underlying code of the global economy for decades.

But during his keynote on the second morning, Chairman Sheng directly pierced through an extremely cold reality: in this super cycle, the priority of "national security" has completely overridden "corporate profitability". In the past, the free market was the default operating system, but now, "industrial policy" dominated by national will has returned, such as the US CHIPS Act, which is meant to forcefully build a fortress regardless of the exorbitant cost. Previously, entrepreneurs only had to worry about making and selling good products; now, enterprises are forced to foot the bill for the security defense lines between great powers.

This is exactly like "Overfitting" when we train models. The global supply chain was originally a model with extremely strong generalization capabilities that could automatically find the global optimal solution. But now, superpowers are forcefully pulling this model for absolute security, demanding it to reach 100% accuracy on the highly niche validation set of "geopolitical security". The result is a plummeting efficiency of the entire system, forcibly partitioning the interconnected tech world into incompatible local networks. For enterprises venturing overseas, the objective function to optimize is no longer just profit, but exponentially increasing the system's "redundancy" and "fault tolerance" in a hostile and uncertain environment.

### **4. The "Rule of Thirds": A Hedging Algorithm Against System Entropy**

At the end of the same sharing, Chairman Sheng talked about global asset allocation and threw out an extremely cold but wise "wealth algorithm".

The most classic Jewish asset allocation model is very clear: one-third in real estate for a baseline, one-third kept as highly liquid assets like cash or gold, and the remaining one-third invested in high-risk main businesses or new investments.

His observation of Chinese entrepreneurs is even more piercing: Chinese people often like to leave all their resources to the next generation. But if that high-risk third, which requires extreme acuity, is handed to a "second generation" lacking capability, the entire system will inevitably head towards collapse and decline. Therefore, the smartest risk control algorithm is to decisively hand this high-risk third to capable "outsiders", forcibly integrating external high-quality variables into one's own closed management system.

Thinking about it carefully, is this not the ultimate method we use in system architecture design to fight against "entropy"? According to the second law of thermodynamics, a closed system inevitably moves towards chaos and disorder. Only by breaking the enclosure of family style and continuously introducing excellent external energy and algorithms can the system maintain long-lasting vitality and iterative capabilities.

### **5. Nanoscale Copper Wires and Oxidation: The "Physical Dead End" of Advanced Packaging**

During the noon session of the second day, Ms. Sun from WafTech shared the industrial reality of Penang's semiconductor sector, and a hardcore technical detail left a deep impression on me.

The underlying code of Penang's semiconductor industry has been written for 50 years, capturing 13% of the global market share in testing and packaging with a highly mature supply chain. However, when the industry attempts to leap to "advanced packaging" at the very top of the value chain, it is deadlocked by the most basic physical and chemical reactions. Traditional packaging mostly involves aluminum wire bonding, which is relatively forgiving of the environment; but in current advanced packaging, the bare surface of the chip is entirely copper. During docking, stacking, and transmission, the copper must never be exposed to air and oxidize, requiring the entire logistics scheduling and equipment handover to be conducted in an oxygen-free (pure nitrogen) environment.

When we solder microcontrollers and sensor modules on our workbenches, who cares about air oxidation? But in the nanoscale world, the most inconspicuous environmental parameters themselves become fatal variables. Facing such harsh requirements of the micro-environment, traditional packaging plants are powerless to upgrade and can only coast on decades of past achievements. In this highly segmented dimension, a small local enterprise that can push physical limits to build an "oxygen-free wafer sorter" can instead tear open a survival rift under the shadows of multinational giants. Grand industrial upgrades and capital frenzies ultimately have to land precisely on solving the most microscopic physical pain points.

### **Final Thoughts**

Observing these sessions over the past few days was like installing a macro-perspective patch into the operating system of my brain.

I used to feel that writing a data parsing algorithm or figuring out a hardware circuit wiring meant grasping some absolute determinism. But looking at the chessboard of great power games and global capital flows, there is absolutely no absolute certainty in the world. Whether it is nanoscale oxygen-free packaging or ASEAN finding life in multicultural clashes, the underlying layers are actually following some philosophical dynamic balance.

This world is an incredibly massive, noisy, and unwritten-rule-filled complex system. What we can do is perhaps maintain the "robustness" of our own line of code within this system, feeling a little less anxiety about involution, and harboring a bit more expectation for the sparks colliding from diverse systems. Looking back, those bigwigs I casually chatted with during the coffee breaks might be facing real-world puzzles ten thousand times more complex than code bugs every single day. And I, by a lucky coincidence, got to preview the game interface of top-tier players in advance.
